Data Science Methodology

How we transformed 102,655 raw transaction lines into 6 actionable customer profiles. Every design decision — from feature selection to algorithm choice to K selection — is documented below.

The Business Question

Starting point: 21,477 customers, 102,655 transactions across 24 months (March 2023 – February 2025), €11.96M in observed revenue. The data exists — but it tells nothing by itself. The question Jakala needed answered was: do these customers all behave the same, or are there structurally different groups that require different commercial approaches?

If distinct groups exist, the company can stop applying a uniform marketing strategy to a non-uniform customer base — and instead allocate budget, messaging, and effort based on actual behavioral differences. The challenge: finding those groups without imposing assumptions about what they should look like.

The 7-Phase CRISP-DM Pipeline

The project followed the Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM), with 7 sequential phases from raw data to financial output:

Feature Engineering — 32 Behavioral Signals

Raw data: one row = one transaction line item. Each customer was aggregated into a single behavioral vector of 32 features across 6 dimensions. The principle: every feature must encode a distinct behavioral signal that could meaningfully separate different types of customers.

👤

Algorithm Selection — Why UMAP + GMM?

All major clustering families were tested on the actual data. The final choice was not a default — it was the result of systematically ruling out alternatives that failed on this specific dataset:

Algorithm Test Result Verdict Why it failed (or was limited)
K-Means K=4, Silhouette 0.18 ✗ Rejected Assumes spherical, equal-size clusters — violated by behavioral data. Sensitive to outliers. Assigns hard membership with no confidence score. Elbow plot showed no clear separation beyond K=4.
DBSCAN 2 clusters + 56 noise ✗ Rejected "Hairball problem": customer behavioral data has near-uniform density — no natural density gaps exist. Algorithm collapses 99.7% of customers into one mega-cluster regardless of eps tuning.
OPTICS 1 cluster + 91% noise ✗ Rejected Same density issue as DBSCAN, despite automatic epsilon detection. 19,783 of 21,428 customers classified as noise (xi=0.05, min_cluster_size=0.05). Unusable at this scale.
Hierarchical (HC) K=4, Silhouette 0.177 ~ Exploratory only Computationally viable at N=21K with Ward linkage. Evaluated for K∈{3,4,5,6}. Best silhouette at K=3 (0.180). Useful for initial exploration but dendrogram cut is subjective and hard to justify at this scale.
UMAP → GMM K=6, confidence >0.80 ✓ Selected UMAP resolves the density problem by revealing non-linear structure invisible to Euclidean-distance methods. GMM then fits probabilistic ellipsoidal envelopes: each customer receives a cluster confidence score, enabling soft assignment and uncertainty quantification.

UMAP — Dimensionality Reduction from 32D to 2D

Standard clustering algorithms operate in Euclidean space. On high-dimensional behavioral data (32 features), Euclidean distance becomes unreliable — a phenomenon known as the "curse of dimensionality." UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) solves this by learning the non-linear manifold structure of the data before clustering.

Input
21,477 customers × 32 behavioral features. Preprocessed with RobustScaler to handle the skewed distributions typical of spending data. High-dimensional space: not directly visualizable.
→
UMAP Projection
Non-linear manifold learning. Constructs a fuzzy topological representation and optimizes a low-dimensional embedding that preserves local neighborhood relationships. Customers who are behaviorally similar end up geometrically close in the 2D projection.
→
Output
2D scatter where 6 visual clouds emerge — each corresponding to a distinct behavioral pattern. GMM is then fitted in UMAP space, placing probability envelopes around each cloud. The result: cluster membership + confidence score per customer.

Why not PCA? PCA is a linear transformation — it flattens curved behavioral manifolds and loses the non-linear neighborhood structure that defines real customer clusters. UMAP preserves local topology; PCA does not.

Choosing K — BIC Elbow Detection + Operational Constraint

The number of clusters K is not an input to be guessed — it must be derived from the data and validated against business constraints. We used two independent criteria that both converged on K=6:

Problem: Pure BIC Minimization Fails

Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) penalizes model complexity to prevent overfitting. On continuous behavioral data, however, BIC can decrease monotonically as K increases. Running K = argmin(BIC) on our data returned K=10 — producing 4 clusters all labeled "one-timer" and 2 labeled "mid-tier." Statistical optimization without judgment is not a solution.

Fix: BIC Elbow Detection

Instead of the global minimum, we detect the last inflection point — the K where the marginal BIC improvement drops below a threshold relative to the first big drop:

drops = [BIC[k-1] - BIC[k] for k in K_range]
threshold = 0.20 × drops[0]
K_elbow = last k where drop ≥ threshold

Beyond this point, adding clusters captures noise rather than genuine behavioral differences.

Validation: Operational Cap K ≤ 6

A statistically valid K=10 segmentation is commercially unworkable. Industry benchmark: beyond 6 distinct segments, a marketing team cannot design, execute, budget, and measure genuinely different campaigns for each group. K=6 satisfies both the BIC elbow criterion and this operational constraint — a convergence that validates the choice from two independent angles.

Statistical Validation of the Final Model

The final K=6 GMM solution was evaluated using four complementary metrics. No single metric is sufficient — each measures a different aspect of cluster quality:

Silhouette Score
Range: [−1, 1] · Higher is better
For each customer, measures how similar they are to their own cluster vs. the nearest neighboring cluster. A score above 0.2 indicates meaningful separation between groups — not just arbitrary partitioning.
Davies-Bouldin Index
Range: [0, ∞] · Lower is better
Computes the average ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster separation for each cluster pair. Low values indicate compact clusters that are far apart — the ideal configuration for marketing segmentation.
Calinski-Harabasz Index
Higher is better · No upper bound
Ratio of between-cluster dispersion to within-cluster dispersion. High values indicate dense, well-separated clusters. Evaluated across K=3 to K=10 to confirm K=6 as an inflection point in the curve.
GMM Posterior Confidence
Per-customer probability score
Each customer receives a posterior probability for cluster membership (0 to 1). Average confidence above 0.80 across all 6 clusters confirms that the GMM found genuine structure — not random partitions with ambiguous boundaries.

The 6 Customer Profiles

Each profile is derived from the actual behavioral averages of its cluster members — no assumptions, no guesswork. KPIs are real aggregates from customer_segmentation_results.csv.
The "So What?" framework translates each behavioral pattern into a concrete commercial decision.

Note on cluster consolidation: The initial model produced 10 groups. Four clusters (2, 3, 6, 8) shared overlapping behavioral profiles — low frequency, high inactivity, low average spend — and were consolidated into Dormant Potential. Two mid-tier clusters (0, 9) with near-identical KPIs were merged into Rising Stars. Merging was based on feature-space proximity confirmed by pairwise Euclidean distance in UMAP coordinates. The result: 6 genuinely distinct segments, each requiring a different marketing lever.
